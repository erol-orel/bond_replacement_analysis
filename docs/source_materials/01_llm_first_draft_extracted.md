Executive Summary

This report addresses a structural problem facing CHF-denominated balanced portfolios: in a zero or negative interest rate environment, a 42% allocation to AAA–BBB sovereign bonds delivers near-zero or negative real return while still consuming the largest single block of the portfolio. With the SNB policy rate at 0.0% as of June 2025 and most economists expecting it to remain there throughout 2026, the problem is once again immediate.

Seven asset classes are evaluated as partial replacements for the bond sleeve, ranked from most liquid (best) to least liquid (worst). The candidates are: Gold, Convertible Bonds, AAA CLO Tranches, Listed Infrastructure, Managed Futures (CTAs), Insurance-Linked Securities (ILS), and Private Credit.

Key findings

Only three candidates — Gold, Managed Futures, and ILS — preserve the bond sleeve's crisis-hedging function across all three historical stress scenarios (2008 GFC, 2020 COVID, 2022 stagflation).

Bonds themselves failed their crisis-hedge function in 2022, when both bonds and equities fell simultaneously.

AAA CLO tranches offer the closest investment-grade, daily-liquid, floating-rate alternative — the cleanest structural replacement for the Swiss bond sleeve.

Convertible bonds provide higher returns but fail the variance-covariance test — their correlation with equities rises in both bull markets and crashes.

Private credit offers attractive yield but its 4–7 year illiquidity caps prudent allocation; reported low volatility is partly an artefact of mark-to-model NAV smoothing.

Recommendation

Replace approximately 20% of the original 42% bond sleeve (i.e., 20 percentage points of the portfolio) with a diversified mix of seven alternatives. This raises expected return, preserves daily liquidity for the bulk of the allocation, and improves crisis-hedging by adding regime-robust diversifiers (gold, CTAs, ILS) that bonds alone failed to provide in 2022.

#1 — Gold (Physical-Backed ETFs, CHF Share Class)

Liquidity rating: ★★★★★ — Intraday, exchange-traded, deep secondary market

What it is

Allocated physical gold accessed via Swiss-domiciled ETFs (ZKB Gold ETF, UBS ETF Gold CHF-Hedged, Swisscanto Gold). Switzerland is the world's gold refining hub — natural CHF access. Spot gold market is among the deepest financial markets globally; ZKB Gold ETF AUM exceeds CHF 10 billion.

Return in low-rate environments (2015–2021)

Approximately +7% to +10% annualised in CHF terms. Gold has a strong inverse relationship with real rates: the lower real rates go, the lower the opportunity cost of holding gold, and the more attractive it becomes. NIRP environments are gold's textbook bull case. From January 2024 to early 2026, gold has rallied substantially as rates have been cut globally.

Variance-covariance fit

Similarities with bonds

Powerful crisis hedge — gold rallied during 2008, 2011 sovereign crisis, 2020 COVID, and held value in 2022. Often a stronger equity-crisis hedge than bonds in inflation-driven crises (preserves F1).

Negative real-rate sensitivity — benefits from the same NIRP environment that destroys bond returns.

No credit risk, no default risk — analogous to AAA sovereign in credit profile.

Where it differs

No income / no carry — fails F2 entirely. Gold pays nothing; total return depends solely on price appreciation.

High volatility — σ of 14–18% is closer to equities than bonds; weakens F3 (volatility damping).

Speculative price risk — short-term moves dominated by sentiment, USD strength, and real-rate expectations rather than fundamentals.

Storage / management cost embedded in ETF (~0.15–0.40% annually); CHF hedging on USD-spot products adds ~1.0% in current rate differentials.

Best use

Allocate 3–6% of portfolio as a partial substitute for the world bond sleeve. Gold is best understood as a regime-shift hedge, not a structural bond replacement. In a stagflation scenario (high inflation + low growth), gold dramatically outperforms the entire bond sleeve.

#2 — Convertible Bonds (Global, CHF-Hedged)

Liquidity rating: ★★★★★ — Daily exchange / OTC; deep institutional market

What it is

Hybrid securities issued as corporate bonds with an embedded equity-conversion option. They behave like bonds when equity is far from the conversion strike (high "bond floor") and progressively more like equities as the underlying stock rises. Global universe ~USD 400 billion, predominantly USD/EUR-denominated. Benchmarks: Refinitiv Global Convertible Bond Index, ICE BofA G300. CHF-hedged access via Lombard Odier Asset Management, UBP, Mirabaud, Schroders, NN IP, and several UCITS ETFs.

Return in low-rate environments (2015–2021)

Approximately +6% to +9% annualised (CHF-hedged), versus near-zero for sovereigns. Convertibles particularly benefit from low rates because: (i) the bond floor remains protective even at low yields, (ii) the embedded equity option gains value, and (iii) issuers (often growth/tech companies) thrive in low-discount-rate environments. 2020 was an exceptional year (+25% globally) due to the tech rally.

Variance-covariance fit

Similarities with bonds

Legal structure is a bond — coupon and par redemption at maturity if not converted.

Bond floor provides asymmetric downside protection versus equities.

Predictable coupon income (preserves F2 partially).

Daily liquid in exchange-listed and OTC markets.

Lower duration than straight bonds — actually a partial advantage in rising-rate phases.

Where it differs — and why it fails the variance-covariance test

High and asymmetric equity correlation: correlation rises in bull markets (when convexity engages on the upside) AND in severe crashes (when the bond floor fails to cushion fully). The net diversification benefit collapses precisely when needed most — exact opposite of F1.

Volatility sits between bonds and equities — but "worst-of-both-worlds" in stress.

Issuer universe is heavily skewed toward growth, tech, and sub-investment-grade companies — factor tilt, not a balanced corporate exposure.

Currency hedging required for the predominantly USD/EUR universe.

In 2008, global convertibles fell ~33% — no defensive properties in a true credit/equity crisis.

Best use

Allocate 3–5% as a return enhancer, not a diversifier. Honest framing: convertibles are equity-substitute with downside cushioning, not a bond substitute. They will not preserve F1 in a market stress.

#3 — Senior AAA–AA CLO Tranches (UCITS-Accessible)

Liquidity rating: ★★★★★ — Daily via UCITS ETFs; bid/ask reasonable in normal markets

What it is

Senior tranches of Collateralised Loan Obligations — structured pools of 150–250 senior secured leveraged loans, with the AAA tranche enjoying 30–40% subordination protection. No AAA CLO tranche has ever experienced principal loss in the asset class's 30-year history (including 2008–2009). Now broadly accessible via UCITS ETFs: Janus Henderson AAA CLO ETF, Invesco AAA CLO ETF, plus actively managed UCITS funds from Spire, Redding Ridge, Fair Oaks, Blackstone Credit. Floating rate (SARON/SOFR + 130–200 bps for AAA).

Return in low-rate environments (2015–2021)

Approximately +1.5% to +3.0% annualised (CHF-hedged net). In USD terms, a steady SOFR + 150–180 bps, which during NIRP (when SOFR-equivalent was ~0%) translated to a real, positive yield versus negative-yielding Swiss sovereigns. In 2024–2025 with rates at 4–5%, gross yields reached 6–7%.

Variance-covariance fit

Similarities with bonds

Investment-grade rated (AAA–AA) — fits the AAA–BBB mandate cleanly.

Daily liquidity in ETF format — preserves F4 fully.

Periodic income (quarterly distributions) — preserves F2.

Low equity beta in normal markets — preserves F1 for rate-driven crises.

No duration risk — directly solves the NIRP problem without giving up credit quality.

Where it differs

Spread-widening risk in stress: even AAA CLOs widened ~150 bps in March 2020, causing 3–5% mark-to-market drawdowns. Less than equities, but more than sovereigns which rallied in flight-to-quality.

Not a 2008-style hedge: in a financial crisis, all credit instruments correlate up; CLOs would lose value while AAA sovereigns gain. CLOs replace yield, not crisis hedge.

Complexity and opacity — CLO managers' security selection matters; not a transparent product.

USD-denominated universe; CHF hedging cost ~0.5–1.0% currently.

Best use

Allocate 5–8% to AAA CLO funds — primarily as a substitute for the Swiss bond sleeve where NIRP impact is most acute. The closest liquid, investment-grade, daily-traded substitute available.

#4 — Listed Infrastructure (Global Core, CHF-Hedged)

Liquidity rating: ★★★★ — Daily exchange-traded; concentrated in mid/large-cap names

What it is

Equity in regulated/contracted real-asset operators — toll roads, airports, electricity grids, water utilities, pipelines. Global universe ~USD 1.5 trillion market cap. Benchmarks: FTSE Global Core Infrastructure 50/50, S&P Global Infrastructure. CHF-hedged access via iShares, First Sentier, CBRE Clarion, Zurich Insurance AM, Swiss Life AM, Lazard.

Return in low-rate environments (2015–2021)

Approximately +6% to +9% annualised (CHF-hedged), with revenues typically CPI-indexed via regulatory frameworks (RAB-based regulation in Europe) or escalator clauses in concession contracts. Real return preservation in a way bonds cannot match.

Variance-covariance fit

Similarities with bonds

Stable, contractual, long-duration cash flows — economically akin to inflation-linked bonds.

Low cyclicality — utilities and toll roads continue earning in recessions.

Positive inflation linkage — solves the bond sleeve's core weakness.

Daily liquid (preserves F4).

Where it differs

σ of 8–12% materially weakens F3 (volatility damping).

Equity beta rises in risk-off events — March 2020 drawdown was ~28%, comparable to broad equities. F1 partially compromised.

Increases overlap with the existing 5% real estate sleeve — concentration in "real assets" factor.

Political / regulatory risk: tariff caps, concession renegotiations, nationalisation (UK water, Southern Europe utilities).

Currency hedging on global universe.

Best use

Allocate 6–10% as a substitute for a portion of the world bond sleeve. Effective yield-and-inflation-hedging tool but not a true bond replacement for crisis hedging.

#5 — Managed Futures / Trend-Following (Liquid CTAs)

Liquidity rating: ★★★★ — Daily or weekly via UCITS; underlying is the most liquid futures markets in the world

What it is

Systematic strategies trading liquid global futures across equities, rates, currencies, and commodities, taking long or short positions based on trend signals. Key managers in UCITS format: Man AHL, Lynx, Winton, Aspect, Lyxor/Amundi, BlueTrend. Famous for delivering positive returns precisely when bonds and equities fall together — the SG Trend Index returned +27% in 2022 while a 60/40 portfolio lost ~17%.

Return in low-rate environments (2015–2021)

Approximately +3% to +7% annualised, depending on the trending nature of markets. CTAs tend to underperform in choppy, range-bound markets and excel in strongly-trending environments. The 2015–2020 period was mixed; 2022 was their best year in two decades.

Variance-covariance fit

Similarities with bonds

Genuine crisis-alpha — the only asset class to consistently rally in extended drawdowns (positive in 2000–2002, 2008, 2022). Arguably better than bonds at F1 in inflation-driven crises.

Near-zero average correlation with equities AND bonds — true diversifier.

Daily-traded UCITS structures preserve F4.

Liquid underlying (futures markets dwarf cash bond markets).

Where it differs

No income / no carry — fails F2. Returns are pure capital appreciation/depreciation.

Higher volatility — σ targeted at 8–14% by most managers; weakens F3.

Negative skewness possible in regime shifts — trend strategies suffer when markets sharply reverse (e.g., 2009 rebound, 2023 H1 reversal).

Performance is highly manager-dependent; dispersion across CTA funds is wide.

Fee structures historically high, though UCITS versions now offer 1.0–1.5% TER.

Best use

Allocate 3–6% as a structural diversifier. The CTA sleeve is conceptually different from bonds — it is not buying yield, it is buying uncorrelated drawdown protection. In academic terms, CTAs deliver positive convexity to volatility and left-tail protection that no bond can replicate.

#6 — Insurance-Linked Securities (ILS) / Catastrophe Bonds

Liquidity rating: ★★★ — Monthly subscription/redemption; secondary market thin

What it is

Bonds whose principal repayment is contingent on the absence of defined natural catastrophes (hurricanes, earthquakes, floods). Returns are generated by insurance premium spread, structurally independent of interest rates, credit cycles, and economic growth. Switzerland (Zurich) is one of the two global ILS hubs alongside Bermuda — natural institutional fit. Managers domiciled locally include Twelve Capital, LGT ILS Partners, Plenum Investments, Solidum.

Return in low-rate environments (2015–2021)

Approximately +3.5% to +6.0% annualised (USD-hedged to CHF). Spread is set by reinsurance market dynamics and is entirely decoupled from monetary policy.

Variance-covariance fit

* Important non-Gaussian tail risk caveat (see below)

Similarities with bonds

Near-zero correlation with all other portfolio sleeves — best F1 preservation for equity-driven crises.

Periodic premium income — preserves F2.

Low reported σ — preserves F3.

No duration / interest rate sensitivity — the cleanest structural escape from NIRP.

Where it differs

Catastrophe tail risk is non-normal: a major hurricane season or Cascadia earthquake can trigger 15–30% losses in a single year. Standard mean-variance frameworks systematically understate this risk. A Master of Finance paper should use CVaR at 99% or scenario simulation, not σ.

Does not hedge a 2008-style financial crisis — ILS is neutral, not positive, in non-catastrophe equity crashes.

Monthly liquidity at best — partial F4 compromise.

USD-dominated universe; CHF hedging required.

Best use

Allocate 5–8% primarily replacing the Swiss bond sleeve. The structural diversification quality is the strongest of any candidate, but liquidity and tail risk constraints cap the prudent allocation size.

#7 — Private Credit / Senior Secured Direct Lending (LEAST LIQUID)

Liquidity rating: ★ — 4–7 year lock-up, capital calls, no secondary market

What it is

Floating-rate, senior secured loans to non-listed European mid-market companies. DACH region focus is natural for CHF portfolios. Swiss/European managers: Partners Group (Baar), Adveq, Patrimonium, Kartesia, Permira Credit, Ares.

Return in low-rate environments (2015–2021)

Approximately +4.5% to +7.0% net annualised = SARON (~0%) + 500–650 bps spread + arrangement fees. The floating-rate structure means spread income is decoupled from the level of risk-free rates.

Variance-covariance fit

Similarities with bonds

Senior secured creditor priority.

Periodic distributions (preserves F2).

Floating-rate structure improves on fixed bonds in rising-rate phases.

Where it differs

Illiquidity is severe — not redeemable for 4–7 years; capital calls over the first 2–3 years. Catastrophic failure of F4.

Sub-investment-grade credit risk — meaningfully worse than AAA–BBB sovereign.

Reported low σ is partially fictitious.

Recession scenarios will cause real, lagging losses (default rates 5–10% with 40–60% recoveries in stress).

Constrained by Swiss BVG/OPP2 alternative-investment caps.

Best use

Allocate 3–5% as a yield-enhancement satellite, not a structural replacement. Pair with liquid alternatives so the overall allocation maintains rebalancing flexibility.

VII. Proposed Reallocation — Target Portfolio

The proposed allocation replaces approximately 20 percentage points of the original 42% bond sleeve. The remaining 22% bond allocation is intentional — sovereign bonds remain the only reliable hedge in a 2008-style flight-to-quality. Cutting bonds to zero would worsen, not improve, true crisis performance.

Rationale

20 percentage points of the original 42% bond sleeve are replaced — the part most exposed to NIRP.

22% bonds retained — sovereign bonds remain the only reliable hedge in a 2008-style flight-to-quality. No replacement fully replicates this. Cutting bonds to zero would worsen crisis performance.

Liquidity profile preserved — only 5% (ILS + Private Credit) is less than daily-liquid, well within Swiss institutional norms (BVG/OPP2 limits).

Income retained — CLOs, infrastructure, ILS, private credit, and convertibles all generate yield, replacing the lost coupon income.

Crisis hedging strengthened — Gold + CTAs + ILS together provide hedging against rate-driven, equity-driven, AND inflation-driven crises (the trifecta bonds alone cannot deliver, as 2022 demonstrated).

Conclusion

Your current 18%/24% bond split, with the 18% Swiss bond sleeve sitting on a 10-year yield of approximately 0.45% in a 0% policy-rate environment, is structurally identical to the NIRP problem of 2015–2021.

The best academic and practical response is not to abandon bonds, but to replace approximately half of the sleeve with a diversified set of seven liquid-to-semi-liquid alternatives that collectively:

Deliver higher expected return across the typical low-rate environment;

Preserve daily liquidity for the bulk of the allocation;

Improve crisis hedging by adding regime-robust alternatives (gold, CTAs, ILS) that bonds alone failed to provide in 2022.

The retained 22% bond core ensures continued protection against deflationary financial-crisis scenarios, where AAA sovereign bonds remain irreplaceable. The added 20% diversifying sleeve protects against the inflationary, regime-shift, and tail-risk scenarios where bonds have repeatedly failed.
